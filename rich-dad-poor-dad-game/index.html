Rich Dad Poor Dad Game

People search for a "Rich Dad Poor Dad game" and usually mean one of three things: the Cashflow board game, a digital version of it, or any game that teaches the book's one useful idea. This page is about the third one — and MY LIFE is a free, single-player browser game built on that idea. You start at 18 with $1,000, no job and no assets, and the whole game is a monthly argument between what pays you and what bills you.

The one rule the book actually teaches

Strip away the anecdotes and Rich Dad Poor Dad makes a single mechanical claim: an asset puts money in your pocket every month, and a liability takes money out. A rental that clears $200 after the mortgage is an asset. The car that costs $400 a month to insure, fuel and park is a liability, no matter how it looks in the driveway. Everything else in the book — the four quadrants, the rat race, "pay yourself first" — is downstream of that one line.

That is also the only rule MY LIFE enforces. Every purchase in the game resolves into a monthly number, and the number is either positive or negative. There is no "net worth" score to hide behind, because net worth is what lets people feel rich while their cash flow is negative.

The four quadrants, and where the game puts you

The book sorts income into four quadrants: E (employee), S (self-employed), B (business owner) and I (investor). The point is not that one is virtuous — it is that E and S income stops when you stop, and B and I income does not. MY LIFE moves you through the same ladder rather than letting you pick a quadrant at the start:

The leap that matters is the one from S to B, because it is the only one that changes the shape of your month. A $10,400 salary is a bigger number than a $900 royalty, and the salary is worth less the moment you cannot work. That asymmetry is the whole lesson, and the game makes you feel it rather than read it.

Escaping the rat race, with an actual condition

The book's exit condition is precise: your passive income exceeds your monthly expenses. Not your salary — your asset income. MY LIFE uses exactly that as the fourteenth of its eighteen goals — the last of the 14 life goals, with four fortune rungs above it, measured in net worth. Until the rentals, dividends and royalties clear your bills, you are still in the race, whatever your bank balance says.

The ladder in between is the part the book leaves vague, and the part a game can make concrete. The 18-goal ladder runs from landing a first job to that final condition, and each one is a legible step rather than a vibe: own your first dividend stock, buy a rental, launch a work at LV5, survive a health hit without selling assets.

How to play in five minutes

  1. Press START LIFE, type a name, pick a face. You are 18 with $1,000.
  2. Tap the gold wheel (or press SPACE) to walk 1–6 blocks. Land on a building and it opens.
  3. Find a job in the first week. Salary is the seed capital for everything else — the book's "pay yourself first" only works if there is something to pay with.
  4. At the bank, turn on pay yourself first: 10% of every salary is swept into the asset fund before bills see it.
  5. Buy dividend stock before you buy anything with a logo on it. Then a rental that clears its own mortgage.
  6. Use a skill until it hits LV5, launch a WORK, and let the royalty arrive while you sleep.

The full control list, the job ladders and the stress mechanics are in the how to play guide. If you want the rules explained without the game, they are in Rich Dad rules — a page about the mechanics rather than about how to start playing.

One honest caveat about this game: it has no multiplayer and no trading, so the deal-making half of the board game is absent. What it has instead is time compression — a month settles every few real seconds, which means a bad purchase shows up in your life in under a minute rather than in round eleven.

What the game takes from the book, and what it leaves out

Takes: the asset-versus-liability rule, the four quadrants, the exit condition, and the distinction between good and bad debt — expressed here as 6% for assets and 15% for consumption.

Leaves out: the book's property-specific advice, its tax arguments, and its more colourful claims about schooling and employment. Those are opinions about the world; a game should teach the arithmetic and let you form the opinions. MY LIFE is also not affiliated with Robert Kiyosaki, Rich Dad Company, or the Cashflow board game — "Rich Dad Poor Dad" is used here to name the idea being taught, the way a book review names its book.

What it adds: a city to walk through, a job ladder with 29 rungs, stress and health as real costs, and a save file in your own browser. Also a rule the book never states out loud — that a collapse in health can force you to sell an asset at the worst moment, which is the most expensive lesson in the game and the one people remember.
